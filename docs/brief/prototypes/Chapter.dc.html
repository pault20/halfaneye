Our eyes

The vertebrate eye was not simply inherited from the paired eyes of our earliest ancestors. The leading account says it was rebuilt from a single light-sensing organ on top of the head.

First published [date]Last revised [date]14 min read

6.1 A fish that barely sees

The hagfish eye has no lens, no muscles to move it and the simplest retina of any living vertebrate. It sits under a patch of pale skin, and the animal behaves as if it were blind.1

1Lamb TD, Collin SP, Pugh EN (2007). Evolution of the vertebrate eye. Nature Reviews Neuroscience 8:960–976. doi:10.1038/nrn2283

For a long time this looked like an eye that had degenerated. Lamb and colleagues argued the opposite: that hagfish branched off before the lens evolved, and their eye preserves a stage our own ancestors passed through about 530 million years ago.2

2Lamb TD, Pugh EN, Collin SP (2008). The origin of the vertebrate eye. Evolution: Education and Outreach 1:415–426. doi:10.1007/s12052-008-0091-2

Lampreys show the same step happening inside one life. A larval lamprey lives buried in mud with an eye much like a hagfish's, and only at metamorphosis does that eye remodel into a camera eye with a lens.3

3Lamb et al. 2007, as note 1, box 1.

6.2 The eye on top of the head

A 2026 review proposes a stranger history. An early ancestor of vertebrates settled into a stationary, filter-feeding life and lost the paired eyes its own ancestors had, keeping only a single light-sensing organ in the middle of its head.4

4Kafetzis G, Bok MJ, Baden T, Nilsson D-E (2026). Evolution of the vertebrate retina by repurposing of a composite ancestral median eye. Current Biology 36(4):R153. doi:10.1016/j.cub.2025.12.028

When its descendants became active swimmers again, the authors argue, parts of that median eye were repurposed into a new pair of image-forming eyes. What stayed in the middle became the pineal gland, which still helps set our daily rhythm of sleep.5

5Kafetzis et al. 2026, as note 4. This is a hypothesis, built from comparing light-sensitive cells across animal groups.

Key evidence

  • Hagfish keep a lensless, muscle-less eye that matches an early stage of vertebrate eye evolution. 6.1
  • Lampreys pass from a hagfish-like eye to a camera eye at metamorphosis. 6.1
  • The leading 2026 hypothesis derives our paired eyes from a single median eye, whose remnant is the pineal gland. 6.2
