## The evidence

The simplest eyes in nature are two cells: a photoreceptor and a pigment cell that shades it from one side. In ragworm larvae that pair is wired straight to the cilia the larva swims with, enough to steer it towards light.[^1]

Blue sea stars have an eye of about 150 units at each arm tip, with no lenses at all. Their image is crude, but animals with intact eyes walk back to their reef when displaced; animals without them wander at random.[^2]

Nilsson and Pelger calculated how many generations a flat patch of light-sensitive cells would need to become a focused camera eye, choosing every assumption to make the process slow. Their answer was fewer than 400,000 generations.[^3]

## Where the claim has a point

An eye is useless without a nervous system that can act on what it sees, and Nilsson and Pelger modelled only the optics. Their figure is an estimate of how fast the eye's shape could change, not a simulation of a whole animal. The living intermediates above are the stronger evidence.[^4]
