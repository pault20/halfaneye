## What the eye is

Look between the tentacles that fringe a scallop's open shell and you will see a row of bright blue dots. Each is an eye about a millimetre across, and a large scallop can have up to 200 of them.[^1]

## How it works

The eye has a lens, but it is too weak to focus. Light passes through the retina, strikes a curved mirror at the back of the eye and is reflected onto the retina from behind. Michael Land worked this out in 1965.[^2]

The mirror is a tiled mosaic of flat, square guanine crystals. Its shape varies across its surface, focusing light from different directions onto one of two separate retinas.[^1]

Light passes the retinas, hits the mirror and is focused back onto them. The lens alone could not form an image.
:::

## What it tells us about evolution

The two retinas use different kinds of photoreceptor. The distal layer has ciliary cells, the family our rods and cones belong to; the proximal layer has microvillous cells, the family insects use. One small eye carries both.[^2]

Scallops are bivalves, which have no head and so no head eyes. Their eyes arose separately, on the edge of the mantle, which shows an eye can appear wherever a lineage gains from seeing a shadow coming.[^3]
